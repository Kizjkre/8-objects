# 8 Objects

*8 Objects* is a participatory sound experience that emerged from collaboration of art
historians, developmental psychologists, and musicians.
The exercise takes about 20 minutes and can be done with groups of any size. Pulling from knowledge of life-span
development, participants locate “eight milestone events” on a timeline (birth, entry to
school, entry to the work-force, marriage, relationship dissolution, having a child, retirement, death).
Pulling from knowledge of the DADA art movement and John Cage’s contemporary music, the events are paired with “eight found objects” that make distinct sounds when dropped on a table, shaken, or squeezed (chestnut, golf pencil, penny, stone, velcro strip, small box of wood matches, small paper bag, twig).
Each participant places the relevant objects along a personal time-line to notate when the events occurred or might occur in their life.
An animated version of the time-line, a horizontal bar that fills over the span of three minutes (about the length of a pop song), is projected on a screen. The timer starts – everyone drops the chestnut onto the table to make a sound representing their birth.
As time progresses, different sounds emerge – each person does something different (individual differences in timing of events) while also participating in the same life span.
When the timer reaches the end, everyone snaps the twig to together in a closing death event. 
Brought together, all the individual actions on objects produce a sonic graph in which we hear both the commonality of life’s progression through milestone events and the uniqueness of individual lives.
Performers/audience, serving as both makers and observers, are immersed in an experience where they (a) contribute information about their personal experiences – data, (b) create a sonic graph that represents those experiences – music, and (c) interpret those experiences through presence – listening.
This simple *8 Objects* exercise is surprisingly provocative and cathartic.
After sonifying her parent’s time-line and then her own time-line, one middle-age woman once noted through tears of relief, “This was the first time in my life that I really understood, you know, really felt how my father’s health changed my own path through life.”
Every time we perform the piece, the music-making process conjures new ideas about life-course data.
